Should You Buy a New Appliance or Have It Fixed by a Professional?

An appliance breaks down and suddenly you're facing a decision: pay for a repair, or start pricing replacements? There's no universal answer, but there is a reasonable way to think it through.

Quick Answer

If the repair costs less than half of what a comparable new appliance would cost, and the unit isn't already old, repair is usually the better choice. Above that threshold, or on an aging appliance, replacement often makes more financial sense.

The Case for Repairing

Repair is almost always the cheaper option up front, and it extends the life of an appliance that may still have plenty of good years left — especially if it's under 8 years old and the problem is a single, isolated part failure. Repair also avoids the hidden costs of replacement: delivery, installation, disposal of the old unit, and the time spent researching and purchasing a new model.

The Case for Replacing

Newer appliances tend to be more energy-efficient, which can offset the higher upfront cost over time. If an appliance is already old, has needed multiple repairs, or the repair quote is starting to approach half the cost of a new unit, replacement often makes more financial sense in the long run.

Pro Tip

Check the ENERGY STAR rating on any replacement you're considering. The efficiency gap between a 12-year-old refrigerator and a new ENERGY STAR-certified model can be significant enough to meaningfully offset the purchase price over several years.

A Simple Rule of Thumb

If the repair cost is less than 50% of a comparable new appliance's price, and the unit isn't already near the end of its expected lifespan, repair is usually the better bet. Above that threshold, or on an appliance that's already old, replacement starts to look more attractive.

10–15 yrs
A well-maintained refrigerator typically lasts 10 to 15 years. If yours is already past that range and facing a major repair, replacement is usually the more sensible financial decision — but a unit at year 5 or 6 with the same problem is almost always worth fixing.

Typical Lifespan by Appliance Type

Appliance Typical Lifespan

Washer / Dryer 10–13 years

Range / Oven 13–20 years

Use this table alongside your appliance's actual age and the repair quote to apply the 50% rule with more confidence.

Other Factors Worth Weighing

  • Environmental impact: repairing keeps an appliance out of the landfill and avoids the resources needed to manufacture a new one
  • Warranty coverage: some repairs may be covered if the appliance is still under warranty, changing the math entirely
  • Availability of parts: older or discontinued models can be harder and more expensive to source parts for
  • Rebate programs: local utility rebates can offset new appliance costs, occasionally tipping a close decision toward replacement
Pro Tip

Before assuming a repair isn't worth it, ask the technician directly how many more years they'd expect the appliance to run after the fix. A good technician will give you an honest answer, even if it means recommending against the repair.

The Pattern of Repeated Repairs

A single isolated repair is usually a straightforward decision. A pattern of repeated repairs on the same appliance — even individually inexpensive ones — is worth stepping back to evaluate as a whole. If you've spent meaningful money on repairs over the past year or two, factor that cumulative cost into the current decision rather than evaluating each repair in isolation.

When in Doubt, Get a Diagnosis First

A professional assessment costs far less than guessing, and it turns a vague decision into a concrete one: here's what's wrong, here's what it costs to fix, here's what a comparable new unit would run. From there, the choice is usually a lot clearer.

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the 50% rule for appliance repair?

If the repair cost is less than 50% of the price of a comparable new appliance, and the unit isn't already near the end of its expected lifespan, repair is usually the better financial choice. Above that threshold, replacement often makes more sense.

How long should major appliances typically last?

Refrigerators: 10–15 years. Washers and dryers: 10–13 years. Dishwashers: 8–12 years. Ranges and ovens: 13–20 years. These are averages — actual lifespan depends heavily on maintenance and usage.

Is it more environmentally friendly to repair or replace?

Repairing generally has a lower environmental footprint, since it avoids the manufacturing resources needed for a new unit and keeps the old one out of a landfill. Replacement makes more sense environmentally only when a much more energy-efficient model would offset that cost over time.

Should I always get a professional diagnosis before deciding?

Yes. A diagnostic visit costs far less than guessing, and turns a vague decision into a concrete one — you'll know exactly what's wrong, what it costs to fix, and can compare that directly to replacement cost.

Do repairs on newer appliances make more sense than on older ones?

Generally, yes. An appliance under 5–8 years old with a single part failure is usually a strong repair candidate, since it likely has many years of life left. The calculus shifts as an appliance ages and problems compound.

What if my appliance has needed multiple repairs already?

A pattern of repeated repairs, even individually inexpensive ones, is a signal the appliance may be entering a general decline phase. At that point, factor in the cumulative cost of past repairs when deciding whether another one makes sense.

How do rebates factor into the buy-vs-repair decision?

Local utility or municipal rebate programs, like those periodically offered through the City of Fresno, can meaningfully offset the cost of a new energy-efficient appliance, sometimes tipping the math toward replacement when it would otherwise be a close call.
