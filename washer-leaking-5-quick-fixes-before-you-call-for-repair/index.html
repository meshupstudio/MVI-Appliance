Washer Leaking? 5 Quick Fixes Before You Call for Repair

Puddles on the laundry room floor are frustrating, but not every washer leak requires a service call right away. Here's a quick sequence to work through first.

Quick Answer

Check supply hoses first, then the drain hose, door seal, and detergent dispenser, in that order. Most washer leaks trace back to one of these four spots and are fixable without a technician visit.

Step 1: Check the Hoses

The most common cause of a leak is a loose or cracked supply hose. Inspect both the hot and cold water hoses behind the machine for cracks, kinks, or bulges, and make sure the fittings are snug at both the wall and the washer. Even hoses without visible damage are worth replacing after five years as a precaution.

Pro Tip

Upgrade to braided stainless steel supply hoses when you replace them. They cost a bit more than rubber hoses but resist bursting far better — a worthwhile trade given how much water damage a failed hose can cause.

Step 2: Examine the Drain Hose

A drain hose can be clogged, detached, or simply positioned wrong. Confirm it's properly inserted into the standpipe or sink drain, not pushed in so far that it causes siphoning, and free of buildup.

Step 3: Inspect the Door Seal (Front-Load Washers)

Front-loaders are especially prone to door seal issues. Check the rubber gasket around the opening for tears, debris, or mildew, and wipe it clean to check for hidden water pooling.

Step 4: Clean the Detergent Dispenser

Too much detergent, or the wrong type, causes oversudsing — and excess suds can force water out where it shouldn't go. Pull the dispenser tray out and rinse it thoroughly, and stick to HE detergent in HE machines.

5 yrs
Washing machine supply hoses should be replaced proactively every 5 years, regardless of visible wear, according to most manufacturer guidelines. A burst hose while you're away is one of the most common causes of major home water damage claims.

Using Timing as a Diagnostic Clue

When a leak happens can tell you almost as much as where it happens. A leak that appears during the fill portion of the cycle often points to a supply hose or inlet valve issue. A leak that only shows up during the spin cycle more often points to a door seal, drain pump, or a load balance problem. Noting when the leak occurs, not just where, gives a technician a real head start if the issue does need a professional look.

Step 5: Know When to Call a Professional

If none of the above solves it, the issue may be a leaking pump, a cracked tub, or a faulty door latch or pressure switch. These require professional diagnosis — and usually professional repair.

Pro Tip

Take a photo of where the water is pooling before you clean it up. Whether it's coming from the front, back, or underneath the machine is one of the first things a technician will ask, and it narrows down the likely cause significantly.

A Word on Prevention

Regular maintenance and the occasional appliance check-up catch small issues like this before they turn into water damage on your floor or cabinets. If leaks keep recurring even after troubleshooting, that's a sign something more significant needs attention.

Why Even a "Small" Leak Deserves Prompt Attention

It's tempting to mop up a small puddle and move on, but water damage from a washer leak often extends well beyond what's visible on the surface. If the laundry room sits on an upper floor or shares a wall with living space, water can seep into subflooring, wall cavities, or even the ceiling below before it becomes obvious — by which point the repair involves far more than just the washer itself. Addressing even a minor, intermittent leak promptly avoids this entirely.

What's Normal vs. What Isn't

A small amount of residual water inside the door gasket or detergent dispenser tray after a cycle finishes is generally normal and not a cause for concern. Water actually pooling on the floor, dripping from underneath the machine, or the exterior cabinet feeling damp to the touch is not normal and is worth investigating using the steps above, even if the amount seems minor at first.

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the most common cause of a washer leak?

Loose or cracked supply hoses. It's the first thing worth checking, since it's simple to inspect and one of the easiest fixes if caught early.

How old is too old for washing machine hoses?

Five years is a reasonable point to replace hoses proactively, even if they show no visible damage. Braided stainless steel hoses tend to last longer than plain rubber ones but should still be inspected annually.

Can too much detergent actually cause a leak?

Yes. Excess suds from using too much detergent, or the wrong type for an HE machine, can force water out through seals and seams not designed to handle that much foam.

Is a leaking washer dangerous?

Water near electrical components is always worth taking seriously. If you see water pooling near an outlet or the machine's control panel, unplug it (if safe to do so) and call a professional rather than continuing to run it.

How much does it cost to fix a leaking washer?

A hose replacement is typically inexpensive. A pump or door seal repair on a front-load washer runs higher, and a cracked tub — while rare — usually makes replacement more sensible than repair.

Can a leak be intermittent, only happening on certain cycles?

Yes, and this detail is actually useful diagnostically. A leak that only occurs during the fill cycle often points to a supply hose or valve; one that only occurs during spin often points to a door seal, pump, or balance issue.

Should I stop using my washer entirely if I find a leak?

For a minor leak clearly traced to a hose you can address yourself, continued careful use while you get a replacement hose is usually fine. For anything unexplained or worsening, it's safer to stop use until it's diagnosed.

Can a leaking washer cause damage beyond just the floor?

Yes, particularly if the laundry room is on an upper floor or adjacent to living space — water can seep into subflooring, drywall, and even ceiling areas below before it's noticed, making prompt attention to any leak worthwhile.

Is a small amount of water after every wash normal?

A small amount of residual water in the door gasket or dispenser tray after a cycle is generally normal. Water actually pooling on the floor, or the machine itself feeling wet on the outside, is not and warrants investigation.
